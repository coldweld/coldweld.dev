In brief

A radiation dose is the energy that radiation leaves in a body, and the game counts it in millisieverts (mSv), once for a whole career and once for a sudden burst.

What the game does

Radiation

  • Radiation is measured in mSv, and every hand carries two doses.
  • The lifetime dose never goes down, and open space adds about two mSv a day to it.
  • Past 1,000 mSv of lifetime dose, a hand asks to go ashore.
  • A solar storm or a radiation belt gives an acute dose in a few hours.
  • Past 1,000 mSv of acute dose a hand is radiation sick, so they fight worse.
  • The body clears acute dose over weeks, and a sickbay or a hospital makes that faster.
  • Each fixed layer of shielding halves a dose, so no shield removes it.
  • A storm is halved by a thinner layer than the background.
  • A storm is weaker far from the Sun.
  • Every screen that quotes a dose also says what it means in years of permitted exposure.

Jupiter'S Moons

  • Jupiter's moons are the exception, and they are not close.
  • Io sits inside a trapped belt worth tens of thousands of mSv a day.
  • Europa is thousands and Ganymede is tens.
  • Callisto sits outside the belt and is quieter than open space.

Why: the real physics or procedure

  • A dose is the energy that radiation leaves in a body, and the sievert is the unit that weighs the damage by the kind of radiation.
  • A chronic dose builds up slowly and raises the chance of cancer, so it never heals.
  • An acute dose lands in hours and burns tissue, so it can make a person sick but the body can heal it.
  • In open space the dose is steady: Curiosity measured 1.84 mSv a day on the way to Mars, with no shielding.
  • The danger is the solar storm: the 1972 event reached about 50 Sv outside a ship and 5 Sv inside a command module.
  • Solar protons are easier to stop than cosmic rays, so a storm shelter of about 25 g/cm^2 makes a storm survivable.
  • Jupiter's magnetic field traps particles, so a belt around Io gives about 36 Sv a day while Callisto sits outside it.
  • A career limit of 1 Sv is the ESA astronaut limit, and the yearly limit for an adult is 50 mSv.

Where the game departs from reality

  • Gray and sievert are treated as the same unit for this radiation (docs/notes/travel.md, DOSE_BANDS).
  • A belt visit counts as an acute dose, although the mirror files belts under chronic. A visit lasts a fraction of an hour, which is the mirror's own idea of acute (docs/notes/travel.md, 'Two doses: LIFETIME and ACUTE').
  • The time in a belt is the one authored number in the model. Three Io visits come to about one sievert by design (docs/notes/travel.md, BELT_APPROACH_HOURS).
  • The clearing rate for acute dose is derived from the chart's recovery times, about six weeks for the 1 to 2 Gy band (docs/notes/travel.md, 'Two doses').
  • The lifetime limit uses the ESA figure of 1 Sv. The NASA career table, which depends on age and sex, is not used.
  • The captain carries no dose, and death at the LD50 level is not modeled (docs/notes/travel.md, 'Not built').
  • The mirror says a crew often gets warning of a storm. The game reports the dose after it lands (gameplay/gameplay.py, applyradiationexposure).

Where the sources are silent

  • The mirror gives no clearing rate in mSv a day. It gives recovery times in weeks and months for each acute band only.
  • The mirror gives no storm size law past 1 AU. The distance scaling for the outer system is an extrapolation (docs/notes/travel.md, 'Solar particle events as a size law').
  • The mirror does not say how long a ship spends in a belt on a visit.

Sources

  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Galactic Cosmic Radiation (Curiosity measurement): Curiosity measured 1.84 mSv a day on the trip to Mars from cosmic rays and solar storms, with no shielding. It flew at the peak of the 11-year solar cycle, when cosmic ray flux is relatively low. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Radiation levels on the way to Mars: A round trip of 360 days in space and 500 days on the surface adds up to about 1.01 Sv. The ESA astronaut limit is 1 Sv. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Solar storms: A shelter of about 25 g/cm^2 keeps astronauts out of serious risk, and 30 g/cm^2 of water is also called enough. Solar particles are easier to stop than cosmic rays. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Space Radiation Risks: GCR vs SFE: One event in 1972, between Apollo 16 and 17, reached about 5,000 REM (50 Sv) outside a ship. About 20 g/cm^2 of aluminum cuts a 5 Sv dose inside a command module to 0.2 Sv. 15 to 20 g/cm^2 of water cuts the cosmic-ray dose to about half. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Gradual SPEs (NASA text): About 10 gradual solar particle events happen each year. They are erratic, run for days, and usually follow a visible coronal mass ejection, so a crew often has warning. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Trapped Radiation (Jupiter dose table): Dose a day: Io 36 Sv, Europa 5 Sv, Ganymede 0.08 Sv, Callisto 0.0001 Sv. Callisto lies at 26.4 Jupiter radii, outside the belt. The single-year limit is 50 mSv and the five-year limit is 100 mSv. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Chronic Radiation Syndrome Chart: The annual limit for an adult is 0.05 Sv. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Acute Radiation Syndrome Chart: Mild acute symptoms appear from 1 Gy and become common near 2 Gy, after a latent phase of 10 to 14 days. The fatality rate in the 1 to 2 Gy band is about 10%, and LD50 is near 4.5 Gy. (all uses)
  • Atomic Rockets, radiation, Acute and chronic exposure (paragraph beginning 'There are two kinds of radiation exposure'): Acute damage heals with time, and chronic damage gradually builds up over a lifetime. Chronic dose raises the cancer risk decades later. "acute radiation damage will heal. Chronic damage does not." (all uses)
